Black-Scholes: price and the Greeks

Black-Scholes computes a European option's price from five inputs: spot, strike, time to expiry, interest rate, and volatility. One equation, no simulation. The Greeks are the partial derivatives of that price — delta per $1 of spot, vega per point of volatility, theta per day of time passing.

Traders watch the Greeks more than the price itself, because Greeks describe risk directly: delta is the hedge ratio, gamma is how fast that hedge goes stale, vega is exposure to a shift in volatility with no stock movement at all. Implied vol (next tab) inverts this formula; Monte Carlo (after that) prices the same thing by simulation.

Implied volatility

Every Black-Scholes input except volatility is directly observable. Implied volatility solves the formula backwards: given a market price, find the σ that produces it.

This is what options actually trade on — not price, but the market's own volatility forecast. The solver uses Newton-Raphson, with vega (from the previous tab) as its slope, and falls back to bisection when vega is too flat to trust: deep ITM/OTM, very short-dated.

Monte Carlo pricing

Black-Scholes has a closed-form solution because its payoff — max(ST − K, 0) — is simple enough to integrate directly. Most payoffs aren't. Monte Carlo prices anything: simulate the random process thousands of times and average the result.

Two uses here. It's a check — this price should converge to the same number Black-Scholes computes, with a shrinking confidence interval to prove it. And it's the technique Project 5's delta-hedging simulation depends on, since there's no formula to fall back on there.

Volatility smile / surface

Every other tab runs on hand-picked numbers. This one runs Implied Volatility's solver across a real option chain: a snapshot of every AAPL quote vol_surface.py pulled from Yahoo Finance, embedded here since a static page can't call a live API. If Black-Scholes's constant-σ assumption were exactly right, every strike would imply the same volatility — it doesn't.

The sliders control the data-hygiene filters real quotes need before you can trust them: a spread cap, a moneyness band, a minimum volume. Loosen them and noisy, illiquid quotes creep into the smile; tighten them and the chart gets cleaner but sparser. That tradeoff — not new math — is what Project 4 is actually about.

Delta-hedging P&L

Sell an option at a volatility you believe in (σhedge), then hedge it by holding delta() shares of stock, rebalanced at every step, along a path that actually realizes a different volatility (σrealized). If hedging were continuous and the two matched, the hedge would replicate the payoff exactly and P&L would be zero. This page lets you watch what happens when they don't.

Set the two vols equal and rebalance often — P&L clusters near zero. Let realized vol run below the hedging vol and the seller profits on average; above it, they lose. Rebalancing more often narrows the spread of outcomes; it does not move that average at all — the bias comes entirely from the vol gap, not from how often you hedge.
